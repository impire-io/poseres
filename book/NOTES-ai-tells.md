# Research notes: what flags text as AI-written

Working notes behind the bans in `STYLE.md`. Not part of the book.
Collected 2026-07-18.

## Surface tells (word/phrase level)

- Overrepresented vocabulary: delve, underscore, harness, illuminate,
  facilitate, bolster, tapestry, landscape, testament, crucial, pivotal,
  robust, foster, leverage.
- Stock hedges: "it's important to note", "it's worth mentioning", "there are
  several factors to consider".
- Stacked transitions: Moreover / Furthermore / Additionally / Consequently
  opening consecutive sentences.
- Vague attribution: "observers note", "industry reports suggest" — claims
  with no named source.

## Structural tells (sentence/paragraph level)

- The not-just pivot: "It's not just X — it's Y", "not only... but also",
  symmetrical two-part sentences that sound wise and say little.
- Rule-of-three adjective lists as a default rhythm.
- Trailing participle summaries: "..., highlighting the need for...",
  "..., underscoring the importance of...".
- Uniform paragraph architecture: topic sentence → two supports → mini
  conclusion, repeated identically for pages.
- Section-final recaps and "In conclusion" paragraphs.
- Em-dash overuse, emoji bullets, title-case enthusiasm in headings,
  meticulous formatting symmetry.

## The deeper tell

Genericity. AI text lacks lived specifics: dates, failures, named people,
numbers with units, stakes, opinions owned by an author. Editors report this is
what they actually key on; the phrase lists are just its residue. Countermeasure
is positive, not negative: write from real events (this project has
`hq/04-JOURNEY/` full of them), state opinions in first person, keep the
irregularity of natural drafting.

Note: detector tools are unreliable in both directions; the goal here is
reading as human because the text is grounded, not beating a classifier.
